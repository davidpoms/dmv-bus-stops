# DMV Bus Stop Intelligence Handbook

## Welcome

DMV Bus Stop Intelligence is a community-powered project that helps identify bus stops where improvements can make the greatest difference for riders.

The project began with a simple observation:

**Thousands of people across the Washington region wait for buses every day without a comfortable place to sit.**

Some riders are older adults. Some are disabled. Some are parents traveling with children. Some are workers waiting after long shifts. Everyone deserves the dignity of waiting for public transit in comfort.

DMV Bus Stop Intelligence creates a transparent, evidence-based inventory of bus stop conditions so communities, advocates, and public agencies can better understand where improvements are needed most.

---

# Our Philosophy

Every rider deserves high-quality public transit.

High-quality transit includes frequent service, reliable service, safe streets, accessible infrastructure, and comfortable places to wait.

A bus stop is part of the transit system—not simply a sign on a pole.

Our goal is to produce reliable evidence that helps communities advocate for better rider experiences.

Every recommendation should be understandable, reproducible, and supported by observable facts.

---

# Our First Goal

DMV Bus Stop Intelligence collects many types of information about bus stop conditions. This information can support future advocacy, planning, and conversations about transit quality.

However, our first goal is specific:

**Identify bus stops where riders may have no comfortable place to sit while waiting for transit, and identify where adding seating may be a realistic improvement opportunity.**

The first generation of recommendations focuses on questions like:

• Does this stop have seating?

• What kind of seating exists?

• Is the existing seating comfortable or only minimally functional?

• Could a bench reasonably be installed?

• Which stops should volunteers verify first?

• Which locations appear to offer the greatest opportunity to improve rider experience?

Other data collected by the project may become useful for future efforts. Deciding how to use that information can remain an ongoing community discussion.

---

# Why Seating?

People often wait several minutes—or considerably longer—for buses.

Standing for those waits can be uncomfortable or impossible for older adults, people with disabilities, pregnant riders, people recovering from injury, or anyone carrying groceries, luggage, or children.

A place to sit is one of the simplest improvements that can significantly improve the transit experience.

---

# Shelter Does Not Equal Comfortable Seating

One important principle of this project is that not all seating provides the same rider experience.

Many bus shelters include built-in benches.

These benches generally satisfy the basic need for seating.

However, many traditional shelter benches are:

• backless

• narrow

• angled

• divided by seat separators

• designed primarily for durability and maintenance rather than comfort

These shelters should not be treated as "no seating."

At the same time, they should not automatically be considered equivalent to a comfortable bench.

Some newer shelter designs improve rider experience by adding features such as greater weather protection and benches with backs. Even these improvements may still include features like seat separators.

Future versions of the project may distinguish between different levels of comfort rather than simply asking whether seating exists.

# A Living Project

The recommendation model can evolve.

Today, the primary question is:

**"Where should new seating be added first?"**

Future questions may include:

• How can uncomfortable shelters be improved?

• Where is weather protection inadequate?

• Where are accessibility improvements needed?

• Where can community stewardship help?

• How should rider comfort be measured?

Not every observation changes today's recommendations.

That does not make it unimportant.

Collecting richer observations today allows the community to ask better questions tomorrow.

---

# How Recommendations Work

The recommendation model combines several categories of evidence.

These include:

• rider exposure

• route connectivity

• existing amenities

• volunteer verification

• seating conditions

The model is designed to prioritize opportunities, not make final construction decisions.

Volunteers should always be able to understand why a stop appears near the top of a priority list.

---

# Data Before Conclusions

Volunteers document observable facts.

They do not decide whether a transit agency must install a bench.

Examples of useful observations include:

• Is there seating?

• What type of seating?

• Does the seating appear comfortable?

• Are there hostile design features?

• Is there room for a bench?

• Would a small site improvement make installation possible?

Recommendations emerge from many observations—not from individual opinions.

---

# Community Ownership

DMV Bus Stop Intelligence belongs to its volunteer community.

The data, scoring methods, and recommendations should remain open to discussion.

Every recommendation model should be explainable.

Every future improvement should be based on evidence.

The project succeeds when people trust both the information collected and the process used to interpret it.
